#. Projections and the Radon transform
The tube and detector rotate around the patient. For each angle θ and detector position s, the measured attenuation is a line integral of μ(x,y). The set of all projections is the <b>sinogram</b>: a point object traces a sine curve.
#. Filtered back-projection (FBP)
Fourier slice theorem: the #D Fourier transform of a projection is a slice through the #D Fourier transform of the image. So projections are first filtered with the |f| ramp, then back-projected. Unfiltered back-projection gives #/r blur.
Window W(f): Ram-Lak (sharp, noisy), Shepp-Logan (sinc), Cosine, Hann (smooth, low noise). Sharpness ↔ noise trade-off.
#. Hounsfield units and windowing
Air −#, water #, fat ≈ −#, soft tissue #…#, bone #…# HU. Because the eye separates only ~#–# grey levels, images are shown through a window (width W, level L): lung W1#/L−#, soft tissue W4#/L4#, bone W2#/L4#, brain W8#/L3#.
#. Artefacts
• <b>Beam hardening:</b> low energies are absorbed first; along long paths the effective μ drops → 'cupping' in the centre and dark streaks between dense bones. Water calibration (BHC) fixes cupping but not bone streaks.\n• <b>Metal:</b> almost all photons are absorbed (photon starvation) → bright/dark radial streaks.\n• <b>Few views:</b> insufficient angular sampling → streak (aliasing) artefacts.\n• <b>Noise:</b> σ ~ #/√(mAs); the fundamental dose–noise trade-off.
#. Dose
Model: parallel-beam geometry (rebinned fan-beam data), energy-integrating detector, scatter neglected.

#. Spin and precession
Hydrogen nuclei (protons) carry a magnetic moment. In the field B₀ a tiny excess aligns with the field, creating a net magnetisation M₀ (M₀ ~ B₀). Spins precess about B₀ at the Larmor frequency.
#. Bloch equations and relaxation
An RF pulse (B₁ at the Larmor frequency) tips M by the flip angle α. Then:\n• <b>T1 (spin–lattice)</b>: Mz recovers: Mz(t) = M₀ + (Mz(#) − M₀)·e^(−t/T1)\n• <b>T2 (spin–spin)</b>: transverse magnetisation dephases: Mxy(t) = Mxy(#)·e^(−t/T2)\n• <b>T2*</b>: field inhomogeneity adds dephasing (#/T2* = #/T2 + γΔB) — a #° pulse reverses this part: the <b>spin echo</b>.
#. Contrast
FLAIR: choosing TI = T1·ln2 nulls CSF (≈ # ms at # T). STIR: a short TI suppresses fat (≈ #–# ms).
#. k-space
Gradients encode position into frequency and phase; the acquired signal is the #D Fourier transform of the image (k-space). The image follows from an inverse FFT. Matrix size sets resolution, FOV sets pixel size; undersampling causes wrap-around, motion causes ghosts along phase encoding.

#. Radioactive decay
Decay is random: one cannot predict when a single nucleus decays, but many nuclei follow the exponential law. Activity is measured in becquerel (# Bq = # decay/s); the old unit is the curie (# Ci = #·#¹⁰ Bq).
#. Generator and transient equilibrium
When the parent (Mo-#) lives longer than the daughter (Tc-#m), A₂/A₁ tends to a constant: transient equilibrium. If the parent is much longer-lived (e.g. Sr-#/Y-#) secular equilibrium arises: A₂ = A₁.
A positron from β⁺ decay annihilates with an electron within a few mm (positron range → resolution loss), producing two # keV photons almost back-to-back (±#°). If two crystals of the ring fire within the timing window (~# ns), a coincidence and a line of response (LOR) are recorded.
• <b>Attenuation:</b> the probability that both photons escape is exp(−∫μ dl) along the LOR — independent of the emission point! That is why PET attenuation correction is done directly from CT.\n• <b>Scatter:</b> Compton-scattered photons create wrong LORs; the energy window (#–# keV) rejects some.\n• <b>Randoms:</b> two unrelated photons fall into the same window; rate ~ activity².\n• <b>NEC</b> = T²/(T+S+R): a measure of image quality.
MLEM is an iterative method that models Poisson statistics directly; it produces fewer streaks than FBP but noise grows with many iterations.

#. Photon beams and depth dose
MV photons interact in water mainly by Compton scattering; energy is deposited by secondary electrons over several cm. Hence the dose is low at the surface and rises to dmax (<b>build-up</b>; skin sparing). It then falls due to attenuation and the inverse square law.
The Klein–Nishina cross section sets the Compton scattering angle and recoil-electron energy. The Monte Carlo simulation follows every photon and electron; the dose converges with statistical uncertainty σ ~ #/√N.
#. Electron beams
Electrons have a finite range: ideal for superficial tumours. Rules of thumb: R5# ≈ E₀/# cm, Rp ≈ #·E₀ cm, therapeutic depth R8# ≈ E/# cm. The small tail at depth comes from bremsstrahlung photons.
#. Protons and the Bragg peak
The stopping power rises as #/β² while the proton slows; most energy is deposited at the end of range (Bragg peak). Range straggling (≈ # %·R) and energy spread broaden the peak. A weighted sum of peaks of different energies forms the SOBP covering the tumour. Beyond the peak the dose is ~#: distal organs are spared.
#. Treatment planning
Dose is calculated with equivalent path length heterogeneity correction (radiological depth = Σ ρₑ·Δl): a beam through lung is attenuated less. Plan quality is judged by the DVH: PTV D9# ≥ # %, cord Dmax < # Gy, lung V2# < # %, mean lung dose < # Gy, heart mean < # Gy (QUANTEC).
#. Radiobiology
α/β ≈ # Gy (tumour, early-responding) and ≈ # Gy (late-responding normal tissue, e.g. spinal cord). Small doses per fraction spare late tissues; high doses per fraction such as SBRT give very high tumour BED but need precise targeting. TCP = exp(−N₀·S).

#. X-ray production
Electrons from the cathode are accelerated by the tube voltage (kVp) onto a tungsten anode. ~#% of the energy becomes heat, ~#% X-rays. Electrons decelerating in nuclear fields emit <b>bremsstrahlung</b> with a continuous spectrum up to E_max = kVp.
When kVp exceeds the W K-shell binding energy (# keV), <b>characteristic</b> lines appear: Kα ≈ #–# keV, Kβ ≈ #–# keV. Intensity ~ (U − #)^#, U = kVp / E_K.
Tube output scales roughly with kVp² and linearly with mAs: #% kVp roughly doubles the detector signal (#% rule).
#. Attenuation and filtration
Two interactions dominate at diagnostic energies: the <b>photoelectric effect</b> (~ Z³/E³, source of contrast) and <b>Compton scattering</b> (~ electron density, source of scatter). The simulation uses NIST μ/ρ(E) tables for every material.
A filter preferentially removes low-energy photons (<b>beam hardening</b>) that would only add skin dose. More filtration raises mean energy and HVL and lowers the output.

#. Image formation
For every pixel a ray is traced from the focal spot to the detector; the path length in each tissue is computed and Beer–Lambert is applied per energy bin. CsI absorption efficiency η(E) = # − e^(−μ_CsI·t). The signal is energy-weighted (energy-integrating detector).
<b>Scatter</b> reduces contrast: the scatter-to-primary ratio grows with field size and patient thickness. A grid transmits ~#% of primary and ~#% of scatter. Collimation reduces both scatter and dose.
<b>Exposure index</b> EI = # × K_detector [µGy] (IEC ##). Deviation index DI = #·log₁₀(EI / EI_target); |DI| ≤ # is appropriate. DI = # means twice the needed dose.
#. Dosimetry
Backscatter factor BSF ≈ #. DAP (Gy·cm²) is independent of distance and is converted to effective dose with coefficients: chest PA ≈ #, abdomen ≈ #, pelvis ≈ #, skull ≈ # mSv/(Gy·cm²). Typical chest PA: ESAK ~#–# mGy, E ~# mSv.
<b>Inverse square law:</b> intensity ~ #/d². Going from SID # to # cm needs ~#× mAs for the same detector dose, but skin dose rises less and magnification drops.

Atoms, nuclei and radiation
The structure of the atom
An atom consists of a nucleus with Z protons and N neutrons, surrounded by Z electrons. The mass number is A = Z + N. Nuclei with the same Z but different N are isotopes, such as ¹²C and ¹⁴C.
Electrons occupy K, L, M… shells. Inner-shell binding energies rise steeply with Z: tungsten's K shell is # keV, iodine's # keV, and the oxygen in water only # keV.
When an inner-shell electron is removed, an outer electron fills the vacancy and the difference is emitted as a characteristic X-ray or an Auger electron. Characteristic energies are element-specific; the tungsten Kα line in an X-ray tube is ≈ # keV.
The electromagnetic spectrum
A photon's energy is proportional to its frequency: E = h·ν. A handy shortcut is E[keV] ≈ # / λ[nm]. Radio waves (MRI), visible light and X/γ rays are the same phenomenon at different energies.
X-rays and γ-rays are the same kind of photon; they differ only in origin. X-rays come from electrons (bremsstrahlung or shell transitions), γ-rays from the nucleus (radioactive decay).
Ionising radiation carries enough energy (≥ # eV) to free electrons from atoms. The top of the UV range, X and γ rays and all fast charged particles are ionising; radio waves and visible light are not.
Direct and indirect ionisation
Charged particles (electrons, protons, α) undergo continuous Coulomb interactions along their path and are directly ionising. Photons and neutrons are uncharged: they first set a charged particle in motion (photon → electron, neutron → recoil proton), and that particle deposits the dose.
The wavelength of a # keV X-ray photon: λ ≈ # / # = # nm — about a tenth of an atomic diameter (~# nm).

Photon interactions with matter
Five interactions
At diagnostic and therapy energies photons interact mainly in five ways: coherent (Rayleigh) scattering, photoelectric absorption, Compton scattering, pair production and photonuclear reactions. Which one dominates depends on the photon energy and the atomic number of the medium.
<b>Coherent scattering:</b> the photon changes direction without losing energy; a small contribution at low energies.
<b>Photoelectric effect:</b> the photon is absorbed completely and an inner-shell electron is ejected. The probability scales roughly as Z³/E³.
<b>Compton scattering:</b> the photon gives part of its energy to a loosely bound electron and scatters. It depends on the electron density and hardly on Z.
<b>Pair production:</b> in the field of a nucleus the photon becomes an electron–positron pair; the threshold is # MeV and the probability rises as ~Z².

Charged particles: stopping power and range
Stopping power
A charged particle makes countless small Coulomb interactions with the electrons of the medium and loses energy continuously. The energy lost per unit path is the stopping power S = −dE/dx (MeV/cm), also given as the mass stopping power S/ρ (MeV·cm²/g).
According to the Bethe formula, the collision stopping power scales roughly as z²/v²: as the particle slows down it loses energy faster. This is the origin of the Bragg peak for heavy particles.
Electrons
Electrons are light, so they scatter through large angles and follow zig-zag tracks. In high-Z media bremsstrahlung (radiative loss) also matters; this is how an X-ray tube produces X-rays. A rough rule: the electron range in water is ≈ E/# cm (E in MeV).
Protons and the Bragg peak
Protons travel in an almost straight line. They deposit most of their dose in the last few millimetres of their range, after which the dose drops to nearly zero. The range is sharply determined by the energy (Bragg–Kleeman rule R ≈ #·E^#). Individual protons stop at slightly different depths (range straggling), so the peak is a few mm wide.
A # MeV proton: R ≈ # × #^# ≈ # × # ≈ # cm. NIST PSTAR gives a CSDA range of # cm, so the rule is a good estimate.
LET and biological effect
Linear energy transfer (LET) is the energy deposited per unit path. Photons and electrons are low-LET (~#–# keV/µm); α particles and slow heavy ions are high-LET (~# keV/µm) and leave dense DNA damage that is hard to repair — hence the radiation weighting factor of # for α.

Biological effects of radiation
DNA damage
DNA is the main target for cell killing. For low-LET radiation about two-thirds of the damage is indirect: OH· radicals from water radiolysis attack the DNA. Single-strand breaks are repaired easily; double-strand breaks (DSBs) are the main lethal lesion.
Oxygen "fixes" free-radical damage, so hypoxic cells are #–# times more resistant (oxygen enhancement ratio, OER). The hypoxic core of tumours is therefore a treatment problem.
The # Rs of radiotherapy
<b>Repair:</b> healthy tissue repairs sublethal damage between fractions (≥ # h apart).
<b>Redistribution:</b> cells move into sensitive cycle phases (G2/M).
<b>Reoxygenation:</b> hypoxic tumour cells become oxygenated between fractions.
<b>Repopulation:</b> the tumour proliferates during treatment; a prolonged overall time reduces control.
<b>Radiosensitivity:</b> the intrinsic sensitivity of each tissue and cell type.

Planning techniques
#D conformal therapy uses a few fixed MLC-shaped fields. IMRT splits each field into small segments to modulate the intensity, with inverse planning optimising an objective function (target doses and OAR constraints). VMAT varies the MLC, dose rate and gantry speed continuously while the gantry rotates, delivering IMRT quality in a few minutes.
Tissue heterogeneity (lung, bone, air cavities) affects the dose calculation: low-density lung raises the dose at depth and widens the penumbra. Modern algorithms (AAA, Acuros, Monte Carlo) account for this.

Proton therapy and the Bragg peak
Why protons?
Protons deliver most of their dose in the Bragg peak and almost none beyond it, whereas photons also deposit substantial exit dose behind the target. Proton therapy therefore cuts the integral dose markedly, especially in children and for tumours near critical organs (eye, skull base, spine).
A single peak is a few mm wide. To cover a target, peaks of different energies are weighted and summed into a spread-out Bragg peak (SOBP). Modern systems paint the target layer by layer and spot by spot with pencil beam scanning (PBS), which enables intensity-modulated proton therapy (IMPT).
Uncertainties
The sharp distal fall-off of protons is an advantage, but range uncertainty is a risk. Because of errors in converting CT HU to stopping power and anatomical changes, a range margin of typically "# % + # mm" is used, and beams are arranged so that they do not point straight at a critical organ.
Clinically, proton doses are prescribed in Gy(RBE) with a constant RBE of #, although the RBE can exceed # at the distal end of the peak where LET rises.

Brachytherapy
In brachytherapy the source is placed inside or next to the tumour. Dose falls steeply with distance (inverse square), giving a high target dose while sparing surrounding tissue. HDR uses Ir-# (T½ # days, mean ~# keV, ~# GBq) in a remote afterloader; permanent LDR seeds use I-# (T½ # days, #–# keV) or Pd-#.

Three fundamental principles (ICRP #)

Area classification and incidents
Areas are classified as controlled or supervised according to expected dose, signposted and access-restricted. A lost source, an unexpectedly high dosimeter reading or a wrong-patient/wrong-site exposure is an incident: it is recorded, its root cause is analysed and, where required, it is reported to the regulator (NDK in Turkey).

Children
Children are #–# times more radiosensitive than adults and have a long life ahead. Size-based protocols ("Image Gently") apply: low mAs, no grid, added Cu filtration, tight collimation; in CT dose should be judged by SSDE. Gonad shields are no longer recommended routinely (AAPM #); collimation is more effective.
Risk communication
Patients and families are often afraid of radiation. A good explanation is numerical yet understandable, compares with natural background, stresses the benefit of the examination, and avoids exaggerations such as "zero risk" or "very dangerous".
